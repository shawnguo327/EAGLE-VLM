\begin{abstract}
Vision-language models (VLMs) are now used as judges to rank image generators, to curate multimodal preference data and to supply rewards for reinforcement learning. A judge that is wrong 30\% of the time is still useful, provided we know \emph{which} 30\%.
For text judges, order-symmetric confidence (query both orders and average the preference) combined with conformal thresholds gives selective judgments with a guaranteed error rate \citep{badshah2026scope,jung2024trust}.
We ask whether this recipe survives the move to vision. Across \TBD{10} public multimodal judge benchmarks (\TBD{10.9k} pairs) and Place Pulse~2.0 street-view perception, with \TBD{three} open VLM judges, we find three things.
\textbf{(i)} Order-symmetric confidence still \emph{ranks} errors: accuracy rises from near chance to about .80 across confidence quintiles.
\textbf{(ii)} In probability space it \emph{saturates}: on street views about 60\% of pairs sit at $\dpconf\approx 1$. This leaves conformal thresholds with little to work with, and at tight risk levels they certify almost no pairs.
\textbf{(iii)} The confidence is blind to \emph{human} disagreement: it does not predict which pairs crowd workers split on.
We propose \eaglev{}, an escalation ladder (pairwise $\rightarrow$ pointwise $\rightarrow$ stronger judge $\rightarrow$ human) whose thresholds are calibrated jointly with fixed-sequence testing, so the error among machine-decided pairs is at most $\alpha$ with probability $1-\delta$. Reading confidence as log-odds removes the saturation. Escalating to a cheaper, differently-biased protocol before escalating to a human raises automation at a fixed risk by \TBD{x--y} points.
For rankings, we use the judge's confidence to choose which comparisons humans label and fit prediction-powered Bradley--Terry/Davidson models. We prove that the gain from any judge is capped by the share of vote variance that is predictable from the pair, which explains when VLM judges can and cannot replace human votes.
\end{abstract}
